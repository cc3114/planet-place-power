From maps to a story map

The question

Madrid's recent heatwaves are not a single thermal event but a layered pattern where climate history, green-cover proxies, and invisible infrastructure shape who feels the heat; the distribution of thermal exposure changes at the boundary of each measurement unit; and the relationship between any two maps in this sequence quietly assumes they share a unit even when they do not.

The method

Subtraction:​ From seven audited entries I kept three maps (Weeks 2, 6, 8) and set aside four (Weeks 3, 4, 5, 7). The kept maps answer "why Madrid cannot cool down" from three different measurement boundaries. The set-aside maps remain in the journal as a record of what was explored but excluded from the argument. Order:​ The sequence is not chronological. It runs: climate baseline (Week 2) → vegetation proxy (Week 6) → infrastructure visibility (Week 8). Each step moves from natural systems to human systems and from coarse to fine to crowdsourced units. Unit-change sentences: Between Map 1 and Map 2: Week 2 uses discrete station points filtered by station ID; Week 6 re-grids the same city to 30-meter pixels. The same "Madrid summer" becomes a point sample in step one and a surface of photosynthetic readings in step two. Between Map 2 and Map 3: Week 6 uses natural surface pixels; Week 8 uses crowdsourced point and line features within an administrative bounding box. "Green space" becomes "mapped asset" — and underground systems disappear entirely.

The evidence

Published StoryMap:​ https://arcg.is/1bP4Hi4 The evidence this week is a sequence of three embedded web maps, not a single map. Each block in the StoryMap links to one ArcGIS Online web map (Weeks 2, 6, 8).

What I found

Read in order, these three maps reveal something no single map can show on its own. Madrid's climate baseline establishes that hot dry summers are structurally built into the city's Mediterranean position, but that baseline stops sixteen years ago before the most extreme recent events that motivated this investigation. The NDVI layer then breaks the assumption that "green equals cool" by showing how managed irrigation produces high photosynthetic index values where soil is too shallow to provide real thermal refuge, while semi-natural woodland drops in index exactly when people need shade most. The infrastructure map adds a third boundary. The power grid that keeps Madrid alive during heatwaves is partially mapped and partially inferred, and the same crowdsourced system that captures transmission towers reliably fails on the underground fibre and telecom assets that actually coordinate emergency response. The pattern that emerges across all three is a relational outcome of climate history, canopy depth, and infrastructural visibility.

What this does not show

This Story Map has clear limits that a reader should keep in view. The climate data stops at 2010 and cannot directly represent the 2022–2025 heatwave years that motivated this investigation; a refreshed baseline is needed before any claim about recent extremes can be grounded in station measurements. NDVI measures photosynthetic radiation, not thermal comfort. High values from irrigation do not equal deep shade, and low values from seasonal drying do not mean a space offers no cooling value, so the vegetation map remains a proxy for ecological function rather than a measure of what a person feels on the ground. The OpenStreetMap infrastructure layer excludes underground telecom and private data-centre buildings, making it a visibility map rather than a complete inventory; mirror-server snapshot inconsistency also means feature counts vary slightly between database refreshes. No surface-temperature raster was produced this semester, as in my Week 4's journal entry, I argue that dense historic blocks might be cooler than exposed peripheral fabric, but the claim was not mapped, and a dedicated land surface temperature layer would strengthen this argument in a later revision. Finally, this Story Map references existing web maps by pointer rather than by copy, which means its stability depends entirely on the three underlying maps staying publicly shared; if any source map is later unshared or deleted, the corresponding block will go silent without warning inside the StoryMaps interface.
