Water

The question

This journal addresses two connected water questions for my Madrid site: where local rainwater runoff travels, and what external claims exist over the city’s drinking‑water supply. This analysis relies on ideas of connectivity for catchment flow, adjacency for drainage divides, and proximity.

The data

Two primary datasets are used. First, MERIT‑Hydro (2021) elevation data accessed via Global Watersheds web‑app, no licence required, used at higher‑precision setting to delineate the 790 km² Manzanares catchment. Second, WRI Aqueduct 4.0 Water Risk, CC‑BY 4.0 licence, baseline period 1979‑2019, vintage April 2023, obtained from ArcGIS Living Atlas. It contains two sub‑layers: Baseline Annual (hydro‑basin polygons cut by Spanish administrative borders) and Baseline Monthly (full un‑cut HydroBASINS sub‑basins for twelve‑month water‑stress values).

The method

I input the site requested coordinate 40.593,‑4.128 into Global Watersheds with MERIT‑Hydro higher‑precision enabled. The tool snapped my requested pour‑point 2.24 km away to 40.417,‑3.723 and output a 790 km² watershed polygon. I exported the boundary as GeoJSON and saved the share permalink. Within ArcGIS Map Viewer I opened Aqueduct 4.0 Baseline Annual layer, clicked my site polygon and recorded baseline water‑stress value 3.03 (Extremely High) and polygon area 2014.86 km². My delineated catchment occupies ~39.2 % of this Aqueduct polygon. I then switched to Baseline Monthly sub‑layer and extracted monthly values: maximum water stress 7.389 in August, minimum 0.726 in March.

What I found

From my Part 0 paper sketch, I incorrectly expected Madrid tap water to originate inside the Manzanares catchment and underestimated watershed size. In reality, a major engineered water transfer crosses the natural drainage divide. The Tagus‑Segura Transfer, an inter‑basin aqueduct, moves freshwater from the Tagus River basin outside my 790 km² Manzanares watershed to supply Madrid’s drinking‑water. It has maximum annual transfer capacity of around 1 000 million cubic metres. Information is sourced from Spanish national water authority MITECO and Wikipedia documentation. This human‑built transfer crosses the topographic watershed divide that the MERIT‑Hydro watershed model cannot simulate. My manual estimate, MERIT‑Hydro watershed and Aqueduct HydroBASINS unit do not match. The MERIT‑Hydro boundary follows the Sierra de Guadarrama ridges to the northwest but runs across flat urban ground near Madrid. Within Aqueduct 4.0 Baseline Annual, a single natural sub‑basin is divided by Spanish administrative boundaries, and all resulting polygon fragments hold the identical water‑stress value. Water stress varies sharply across months, reaching 7.389 in August and falling to 0.726 in March.

What this does not show

I am using the delineated catchment boundary at my site to stand for where Madrid’s tap water actually comes from, and it might fail because the watershed calculation only models natural surface runoff and does not represent the Tagus‑Segura interbasin pipeline transfer that supplies much of the city’s drinking water.
